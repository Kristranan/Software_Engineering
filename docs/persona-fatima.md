# Persona: Fatima Al-Sayed

Source: client interview, exported 30 Aug 2026 (`sources/interview-transcript.md`).
Everything below comes from that interview unless marked otherwise.

## Profile

- Role: Cold Chain Program Manager — public health logistics, last leg of vaccine
  distribution (regional depots to rural clinics)
- Experience: about 12 years in public health logistics, the last 4 in cold chain
  management
- Not technical: "I know what problems feel like and what good outcomes look like,
  but I genuinely couldn't tell you how to build something that achieves them."
- Location: a central regional office. She declined to give an exact location; the
  geography that matters is the remote clinics.
- Did not give her age or appearance.

## Context

- Clinics are scattered across remote areas, some hours from the nearest town, on
  roads that become impassable in rainy season.
- Unreliable power and unreliable internet at the clinic end.
- Temperature is recorded on paper strip-chart recorders that must be interpreted by
  hand; different staff read them differently.
- A manually updated shared spreadsheet tracks shipment status; she does not trust it.
- A whiteboard with coloured markers tracks active shipments in her office.
- Small admin team of two people, dedicated but overwhelmed.

## The incident

A refrigerated vehicle's unit malfunctioned during an overnight stop. The vaccines
reached two clinics the next afternoon. The strip chart was ambiguous, the nurses
were not sure how to read it during a busy clinic day, and they administered doses.
Nobody knows how many patients were affected or which ones.

What stays with her: "It's not the incident itself, it's the silence afterwards. No
alarm went off." She suspects it was not the first time — only the first time they
found out. The incident triggered ministry pressure to modernize.

## Quotes

- "By the time I find out something went wrong, it's already too late to do anything
  about it." (her representative quote)
- "The gap between 'we think this worked' and 'we know this worked' is the difference
  between real public health and just the appearance of it."
- "I want to run a cold chain where silence means everything is fine — not silence
  means nobody knows."
- Motivation (short): "Making sure no child receives a vaccine that doesn't work — and
  knowing about it before it's too late to do anything."

## Her day

| Time | Activities | Pain point |
|---|---|---|
| Early morning | Checks phone for overnight messages, contacts depot managers about today's shipments, pieces together yesterday | No visibility of what happened overnight until paper logs arrive, sometimes days later |
| Mid-morning | Tracks drivers who have not checked in, follows up on deliveries, reconciles paper logs with the spreadsheet, drafts updates | Spreadsheet updated inconsistently; strip charts interpreted differently |
| Midday | Tracks arrivals and receipt confirmations, calls regional health officer / ministry, mediates depot–clinic issues | Nurses sometimes send WhatsApp photos of strip charts — only if they remember and have signal |
| Afternoon | Closes out the day, updates the spreadsheet, decides use-or-discard on doubtful vaccines, calls manufacturer hotlines | Viability decisions without reliable, consistent information |
| Evening | Reviews completed vs planned, flags open items, writes incident reports, sends end-of-day summaries | Overnight gap: shipments in cold boxes with nobody monitoring |
| After hours | Emergency calls about breakdowns or refrigeration failures; checks phone at night out of anxiety | No after-hours monitoring unless a human notices and calls |

## What she must get done each day

1. Confirm all planned shipments departed on time.
2. Verify in-transit deliveries had no temperature issues.
3. Get receipt confirmation from clinics.
4. Make pending vaccine viability decisions.
5. Send an accurate end-of-day report to the ministry contact.

"The honest answer is that most of these things don't fully happen on most days."

## Pain points (her ranking)

| Pain point | Impact | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| No real-time temperature visibility during transit | High | Daily |
| Overnight monitoring gap | High | Daily |
| Viability decisions without reliable information | High | Several times a week |
| Strip charts ambiguous and interpreted inconsistently | High | Daily |
| Information arrives too late to act on | High | Daily |
| Nurses have no simple way to flag concerns | Med | Daily |
| Spreadsheet inconsistent across team members | Med | Daily |
| After-hours response depends on someone calling her | High | Weekly-ish |

Also raised: vaccine tolerance rules are not documented for field use, and there is
no way to anticipate risky shipments before they depart (purely reactive).

## Needs (her ranking)

| # | Need | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Real-time temperature monitoring with immediate alerting | High |
| 2 | Offline and local alerting without internet or reliable power | High |
| 3 | Vaccine-specific viability assessment from actual exposure history | High |
| 4 | Clinic-end alerting that needs minimal action from nurses | High |
| 5 | Consolidated shipment tracking replacing the spreadsheet | Medium |
| 6 | After-hours emergency alerting | High |
| 7 | Predictive routing and dispatch support | Medium |

If forced to pick one: need 1, "because that's the one where failure means direct
patient harm with no warning."

## Goals and what blocks them

| Goal | What gets in the way |
|---|---|
| Vaccines arrive within safe range | No transit visibility; ambiguous charts; no overnight monitoring |
| Correct viability decisions | Tolerance rules not available consistently; team decides differently |
| Nurses can flag problems without extra work | Current system needs nurses to interpret charts and report; it gets skipped |
| Accurate ministry reporting | Data from inconsistent spreadsheets and paper logs |
| Prevent problems before they happen | Everything is reactive; route-risk knowledge is never used systematically |

## A good day

- She knows nothing went wrong overnight because she can see it, not because nobody
  called.
- She finds out about problems while there is still time to reroute or retrieve.
- Clinic nurses have what they need with no extra burden.
- She makes viability calls with confidence.
- She can tell the ministry something true.

## People

- Reports to: the Regional Health Director, who answers to the ministry and wants
  visible progress quickly.
- Works daily with: three depot managers (one per geographic zone) who dispatch
  shipments and manage drivers. Their systems do not connect to hers.
- Depends on, without authority: clinic nurses.
- Reporting level: ministry and donor contacts (outcomes and numbers).
- Occasionally: drivers, regional health officers, manufacturer contacts.

## Technology

- Fatima: Medium, leaning Low ("tech-functional"). Phone, email, WhatsApp, basic
  spreadsheets.
- Clinic nurses: mostly Low. Senior nurses uncomfortable with anything beyond phone
  calls. Most have only a basic smartphone; signal is unreliable; some clinics may
  have no computer.
- Depot managers: Medium. Will use what works; will not troubleshoot or read a manual.
- Her advice: "design for the Low end." A simple visual indicator on a physical
  device would serve nurses better than any app or dashboard.
- Devices: personal mobile phone for everything urgent; a laptop at her desk for the
  spreadsheet and reports.

## Behaviors

- Checks her phone before she is properly awake.
- Calls the same depot managers in the same order every morning.
- Keeps a mental list of shipments "in the air".
- Chases unconfirmed deliveries by midday.
- Adds buffer time and uncertainty language to every ministry report.
- Calls manufacturer hotlines for vaccine-specific rules.
- Trusts depot managers most; skeptical of her own spreadsheet.
- Decides mostly by instinct under time pressure, which bothers her.
- Says she is an introvert, intuition, feeling, judging.

## Think / See / Feel / Do highlights

- Thinks: the silence of the incident; whether the system will work for nurses.
- Fears: a system that works in connected clinics but fails silently in remote ones;
  solving detection but never prevention; ministry pressure producing something that
  looks impressive but does not protect patients.
- Feels: baseline anxiety, guilt toward nurses, frustration, occasional helplessness,
  pride when a cycle completes cleanly.
- Publicly vs privately: publicly "modernization is progressing"; privately "we are
  operating largely blind and got lucky."

## Pains and gains (her top 3)

Pains: silence during transit; irreversible decisions with poor information;
solutions that work everywhere except where they are needed most.

Gains: knowing in real time while she can still act; a consistent viability
assessment per vaccine; nurses protected without being burdened.
